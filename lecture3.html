Firmware and Mechanical 101 — polish, box, and what else

Goal. The boards are on order (placed Monday 28 September, back around 12 October), so everything today runs in sim mode on the dev board. You give your block its driver and its app panel, wire one alarm rule, and — in a 40-minute add-on — design a printable box for the instrument from the instructor's Onshape template. The instructor prints all five.

Learning goals: how a block driver and an app panel fit the skeleton · work against hardware that is not there yet (sim mode) · design a printable enclosure from a template · know what to do next with all this.

Prerequisite: Homework 2 done and your zone merged; an Onshape account (free education plan, runs in the browser). Bring your laptop and your dev board.

Mechanical is a short add-on, not a workshop. CAD is Onshape (free education plan, browser-based, nothing to install). The instructor owns a parametric box template that already contains the board STEP and the front-panel cutouts; you copy it, change one thing, export an STL, and the instructor prints the queue on the Bambu. The institute's laser cutter gets one slide as the alternative for flat panels; it is not used for the box.

Topics

The block-driver interface

Every block is a folder firmware/src/blocks/b<N>_<name>/ implementing begin() / loop() / handle(cmd) / status(out). You start from blocks/template/.

The protocol

WebSocket messages {block, cmd, args} in and {block, status} out; one panel module per block in host/pwa/panels/b<N>.js; instrument.py mirrors the same commands in Python.

Sim mode

The SIM flag fakes the hardware so the whole app runs on a bare dev board — you work two weeks ahead of the boards and swap the flag off when they arrive.

One alarm rule per panel

Your block's status → the base alarm engine → relay + notification: B1 "AI1 above a threshold" · B2 "a rail out of range" · B3 "output clipped" · B4 "opto count jumped" · B5 "TRIG edge seen". Every student builds the demo's alarm step personally.

Boxes for electronics

FDM tolerances (holes +0.3 mm), 2 mm walls, heat-set inserts, layer direction, why the PCB front panel makes the cutouts trivial, the STEP export from KiCad.

The Onshape template

Three parameters (height, wall, foot style) and one "make it yours" change — vent pattern, embossed name, feet, a colour note — then export STL to docs/students/<name>/box.stl.

Tips and what else

Bot alerts (LINE/Telegram) from the alarm engine, scripted sweeps from instrument.py, the 60-second video, over-the-air updates (OTA), and where to take this: your own lab's instrument.

Wrap-up (1.5 h, week of 19 October; boards back ≈ 12 October) — Exercise E13

During the week of 12 October the instructor flashes and bench-checks every board (rails, LED, WiFi) and prints the boxes. Then:

  • E13a (0.5 h) Real hardware. Flash your own build without SIM, open your panel, see your block move real hardware (B1 a voltage on AI1 · B2 rail LEDs and the status panel · B3 a sine on AO1 into AI1 · B4 a relay click and an opto count · B5 a TTL line and TRIG direction). Fix one thing with the tutor.
  • E13b (0.5 h) One measured number for your block, written into SPEC.md with how you measured it: B1 noise floor of one input (LSB rms) · B2 the four rail voltages and the USB current · B3 amplitude accuracy of a 1 kHz sine at ±5 V · B4 relay switching time from an opto timestamp · B5 TTL edge rate on a scope.
  • E13c (0.5 h) 60-second phone video of that, uploaded to docs/students/<name>/ and linked from your Showcase card.

Demo week (26–30 October, scheduled 5-minute slots, no extra prep) — Exercise E14

From the class checklist: (1) waveform out, seen on the scope and on the instrument's own input · (2) change it from the phone · (3) alarm on an input → relay → notification · (4) your block: its panel and one thing it does — play your 60-second video if the live part fails.

Score sheet: block works 40 % · PR quality incl. your review of a peer 30 % · demo clarity 20 % · box 10 %.
