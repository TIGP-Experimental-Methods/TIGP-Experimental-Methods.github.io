Workshop 3 — Firmware and basic mechanical design (PCB housing)

Outline.

  • Setup and preparation check — everyone has their laptop prepared and Onshape (a CAD program that runs in the browser, free education plan) or another CAD program ready.
  • Introduction. How much background does everyone have?
  • Mechanical fabrication techniques 101 — a tour of the fabrication facilities at IAMS: FDM and resin 3D printing, laser cutting, water-jet cutter, lathe, mill.
  • Mechanical design 101 — using CAD, the basics: extrudes, cuts, bending, holes, threads.
  • Tips for fabrication.
  • Project 3b: Making a housing for our instrument. Use Onshape (or your favourite CAD program) to draw a housing for your instrument, for 3D printing and/or laser cutting.
  • Continue building your app, firmware, website and videos.

Hard cutoff: Friday 9 October, 2 pm. The final housing files for 3D printing / laser cutting must be in for manufacturing then.

Prerequisite: your section merged before the class-order cutoff; an Onshape account or another CAD program. Bring your laptop and your dev board.

Detailed plan (draft — follows the workshop outline above)

The boards are on order (placed 28 September, back around 16 October), so everything on the firmware side runs in sim mode on the dev board (a build in which the firmware — the program on the microcontroller — fakes its hardware, so the app works on the bare board). You give your section its driver and its app panel, wire one alarm rule, and design the housing.

Firmware and app — your section's driver and panel

The block-driver interface

Every section is a folder firmware/src/blocks/b<N>_<name>/ implementing begin() / loop() / handle(cmd) / status(out). You start from blocks/template/.

The protocol

WebSocket (a live two-way connection between the phone page and the board) messages {block, cmd, args} in and {block, status} out; one panel module per section in host/pwa/panels/b<N>.js; instrument.py (a Python program on your PC) mirrors the same commands in Python.

Sim mode

The SIM flag fakes the hardware so the whole app runs on a bare dev board — you work ahead of the boards and swap the flag off when they arrive.

One alarm rule per panel

Your section's status → the base alarm engine → relay + notification: B1 "AI1 above a threshold" · B2 "a rail out of range" · B3 "output clipped" · B4 "opto count jumped" · B5 "TRIG edge seen".

E11: driver + panel in sim. /tutor L3: copy the template to blocks/b<N>_<name>/; fill in your section's commands (B1 read_all, set_range · B2 rails status · B3 set_dc, sine · B4 relay, opto_count · B5 dio, trig_dir); make the SIM branch return plausible values; add the panel and one alarm rule; flash (write the firmware onto the board over USB); see the panel react on the phone; open the pull request (a request to merge your changes into the shared project; someone reviews it first). The tutor generates the skeleton; you name the commands, write the SIM values, and explain one message round-trip before flashing. Sections that talk to each other are paired (B3 ↔ B1, B5 ↔ B4). Done when the panel controls the section in sim and the pull request is open.

Housing — Project 3b

Housings for electronics

FDM tolerances (holes +0.3 mm), 2 mm walls, heat-set inserts, layer direction, why the PCB front panel makes the cutouts trivial, the STEP export (3D CAD exchange file) from KiCad.

A starting point

The instructor's Onshape template already contains the board STEP and the front-panel cutouts, with three parameters (height, wall, foot style). Copy it and make it yours — vent pattern, embossed name, feet — or draw your own housing from scratch in the CAD program you prefer.

Output

STL (the 3D-print file) and/or DXF (the laser-cutting drawing) to docs/students/<name>/, in by the cutoff. The institute prints and cuts them.

E12: your housing. The template on the projector: copy, import the board STEP, the parameters, one "make it yours" change, export. You do the same in your own account — or design your own. Done when the files are exported.

Presentation and demonstration

  • ~Friday 16 October: collect the manufactured PCBs and housings.
  • Assemble your ESP32, PCB and housing. Flash your own build without SIM, open your panel, see your section move real hardware (B1 a voltage on AI1 · B2 rail LEDs and the status panel · B3 a sine on AO1 into AI1 · B4 a relay click and an opto count · B5 a TTL line and TRIG direction).
  • Test and refine your firmware and app.
  • 26–30 October: present and demonstrate your project, app, website and video — about 15 minutes each. For example, make a video to describe your app and board.
  • Project websites and the wall: each project has its own public repository and project website (GitHub Pages). Project 3 — your instrument: board section, firmware and app panel, housing — is one repository and one website, presenting the whole of it with pictures, renders or video, what it does, what was measured, and links to your pull requests in class-board-2026 and to the class repository (the KiCad files stay in the class repository; your repository presents the work and may hold the housing CAD export, photos and the video); it is one card on the wall. All of them are on the class project wall — see Showcase.

A suggested structure for the demonstration: (1) a waveform out, seen on the oscilloscope and on the instrument's own input · (2) change it from the phone · (3) an alarm on an input → relay → notification · (4) your section: its panel and one thing it does · (5) anything you built beyond the baseline — a data logger, a LINE alert, a PID block, a Python sweep, the tool your lab needed.
