Workshop 2 — Designing printed circuit boards

Outline.

  • Setup and preparation check — everyone has their laptop prepared and KiCad running (the free program we draw the schematic — the circuit drawing — and lay out the printed circuit board in).
  • Introduction. How much background does everyone have?
  • Block assignment — the class board has one section per student: volunteers first, then lots.
  • Electronics 101 — a crash course in electronics to understand what we are building and how it works. Ask your Claude tutor to explain the ideas (for example in Mandarin); the tutor may ask you questions to help teach and check your understanding.
  • Introduction to PCB design with KiCad, automated production and assembly — from schematic to layout to the files a factory turns into an assembled board.
  • Project 3a: Turning our ESP32 into a real piece of lab equipment. Build the skeleton of our class board together; add capabilities and chips to our ESP32 in KiCad. Everyone designs a different section, so that as a team we produce a powerful instrument — chips, front panel and more.

Be creative!

Detailed plan (draft — follows the workshop outline above)

The hardware model: dev board + carrier, assembled by JLCPCB

The MCU (microcontroller, the small computer on a chip) stays on the proven ESP32-S3 dev board (the board that carries it, with a USB-C connector and pins); the class board is a carrier it plugs into — the analog front end done properly, isolation, buffers, robust connectors. One design, one order, one assembled board per student. JLCPCB assembles everything, surface-mount and through-hole; the front panel is a second small PCB. You solder nothing, and the dev board alone keeps running the app as the permanent fallback. Fully assembled, the board costs about US$100 per student.

Electronics 101 — six ideas, each pointed at our schematic

(a) Rails, decoupling, ground

Where the power comes from and how it is cleaned up next to every chip; the one place analog and digital ground meet. Section B2.

(b) What ±10 V and 16 bits buy

Range and resolution on the input and output side, and why the dev board's own ADC and DAC (analog-to-digital and digital-to-analog converters) are not enough. Sections B1 and B3.

(c) The op-amp stage as a black box

Gain and offset without the internals: how the DAC's output becomes a ±10 V swing. Section B3.

(d) Isolation

Relay, opto-coupler, creepage distance: switching and sensing things that must not share your ground. Section B4.

(e) 3.3 V vs 5 V logic

Why a GPIO (a general-purpose pin on the microcontroller) cannot drive 5 V TTL directly and what the buffers do. Section B5.

(f) The buses

SPI and I²C (two common wiring standards for chips to talk to the microcontroller) tie the sections to the MCU; which pins carry the fastest signals in your section.

PCB design with KiCad — the only six operations

Six operations

Place a part and wire it · annotate · Update PCB from schematic · drag footprints into place · route (draw the copper tracks) · DRC (KiCad's design-rule check), then the 3D view. Shown live once on the projector, in the base sheet.

Where parts come from

LCSC part numbers on every symbol (a part's drawing in the schematic); easyeda2kicad pulls symbol, footprint and 3D model; JLCPCB places what LCSC stocks. That is automated production and assembly.

Pull request + peer review

One pull request (a request to merge your changes into the shared project; someone reviews it first) for your section, one review of a classmate's from the checklist, one round of fixes — the same list the instructor uses at merge.

Between workshops — what must be finished for the cutoff (Monday 28 September, 2 pm): E7–E10

Before the next workshop: complete the preparation, improve your apps, build and have fun. The preparation this time: the steps your section needs before the Gerber files go to the factory. A section that is not merged by the cutoff is not on the board.

  • E7 Finish routing. DRC 0 errors, 3D screenshot.
  • E8 One part from LCSC. easyeda2kicad --full --lcsc_id C474881 (the 2-pin screw terminal; or your LED); add it to the project library; swap it in.
  • E9 Open your pull request. Branch (a separate line of work) b<N>-<name>, commit (save a snapshot with a one-line message), pull request with the 3D screenshot; request your ring reviewer.
  • E10 Review your peer, then fix yours. Walk the checklist on their pull request; leave at least two comments; approve or request changes. Then address the comments on your own.

Peer-review checklist (workbook ch. C — the same list the instructor uses at merge): ERC 0 / DRC 0 · LCSC field on every part · values match the PDF · decoupling next to the pins · nothing on layer 2 · nothing outside your zone · pin 1 marked · ground pour joined · B4 creepage ≥ 2.5 mm · silkscreen label on every connector · CI green (continuous integration: the automatic build GitHub runs on every pull request passes).

Class order: your section must be merged before the cutoff, Monday 28 September, 2 pm. Then the instructor merges the sections into main, runs the full DRC, exports the Gerbers, BOM (parts list) and CPL (placement list) and orders the assembled boards, bare boards and front panels from JLCPCB.
