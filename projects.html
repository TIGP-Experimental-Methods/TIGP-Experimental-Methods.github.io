The class instrument: function generator / AWG & lab controller

Everyone builds the same instrument platform — a bench "Swiss-army-knife" every lab can use. In 2026 it is one class board in five sections (blocks), one per student — Project 3a (the board, in KiCad) and Project 3b (its housing). The hardware is deliberately standardised so the workshops, the design review and the class PCB (printed circuit board) order all pull in one direction; where you excel is the software: the interfaces, automation and polish that turn a circuit board into an instrument people love.

What the box does

  • Generates signals: a 16-bit two-channel arbitrary-waveform generator — DC levels, ramps, sines and arbitrary tables to about 50 kHz at ±10 V (AO1, AO2) — plus two hardware-timed trigger/clock outputs at 5 V TTL to tens of MHz.
  • Monitors signals: eight 16-bit ±10 V inputs on SMA connectors (AI1–AI8), with software-selectable ranges down to ±0.64 V for sensor work, logged and plotted. An instrumentation-amplifier / programmable-gain conditioning chain is reserved on the board as an instructor option feeding inputs 7 and 8.
  • Switches things: four low-voltage relays, eight 5 V TTL outputs, two fast opto-isolated inputs and a firmware-switchable TRIG connector (in or out) — alarms, interlocks, sequenced actions.
  • Talks to everything: native USB to Python, WiFi to a web page, your phone and messaging bots. Your phone is the front panel (a small OLED shows status); an optional camera node streams pictures into the same app — one ESP32-S3-N16R8-CAM 開發板 + OV3660 鏡頭 (Jinhua #41395) is available for anyone who wants to try a camera app.

MCU (microcontroller, the small computer on a chip) choice (frozen for 2026): an ESP32-S3 N16R8 dev board with the standard DevKitC-1 pinout and an external-antenna connector (Jinhua, NT$400), socketed on the class board and running the Arduino framework. WiFi unlocks the web/phone/bot directions; the u.FL antenna goes to an SMA bulkhead so WiFi works inside a closed metal or acrylic case; the dev board leaves 31 usable GPIO (general-purpose pins) and an 80 MHz SPI bus (a common wiring standard for chips to talk to the microcontroller) for the converters. The core circuit is standardised so the class order and design reviews stay manageable — but you still do real hardware work on it (next paragraph), and your creativity also goes into firmware (the program that runs on the microcontroller), host software, interfaces and the enclosure.

One class board, five blocks — you own one. The board is a single KiCad (circuit-design program) project in one class repository (a shared project folder whose complete history git keeps, stored online on GitHub). The instructor builds the complete board first — the base (dev-board socket, buses, expansion header, the front-panel PCB with its 12 SMA connectors) and all five blocks — and hands you the full schematic (circuit drawing) as a PDF plus a gapped copy of your block's sheet with a few parts deleted. The blocks: precision inputs (ADS8688, AI1–AI8), power & rails (USB-C / 5 V jack, ±12 V isolated modules, regulators), signal generation (DAC8563 + ±10 V output stages), isolated switching (relays + fast opto inputs) and digital I/O & timing (TTL outputs, fast trigger outputs, the TRIG connector). Your work, each step done once with Claude as your tutor: place the deleted parts from the PDF, fetch one part from LCSC (the parts catalogue of JLCPCB, the factory that assembles our boards) into the library, route your zone of the board (draw the copper tracks inside the region that is yours; ICs and connectors are pre-placed), open a pull request (PR: a request to merge your changes into the shared project; someone reviews it first) and review one classmate's, then write your block's firmware driver and app panel from the template — in simulation mode first, on the real board when it arrives. The merged design goes into the class order on Monday 28 September, 2 pm (a hard cutoff) and is manufactured five times — one assembled instrument each. Your zone, your pull request and your review of a peer are your part of Project 3a; the housing is Project 3b.

Rules: low-voltage only — the instrument runs from USB-C or a 5 V supply, relays switch signal-level and ≤ 30 V DC loads, never mains. Parts JLCPCB-stocked; dev board provided by the course. Cost: the class board is one design manufactured ×5, and the parts were chosen for learning per dollar — about NT$3.2k per student (≈ US$100) all-in: dev board, parts, 4-layer PCB, front-panel PCB and complete assembly by JLCPCB, so there is nothing to solder. Every part must earn its place, and the JLC quote is checked before the order.

Other ideas: add-on hardware for an ESP32-class development board

The class hardware is fixed — one board design, ordered once for everyone; nothing on this list is part of the course or an extension for students. It is here for inspiration only: ideas for add-on hardware that could be attached to a development board like an ESP32 to create other useful tools for the lab — after the course, or in your own work. The class board keeps a 2×10 expansion header, two Qwiic connectors and a few reserved footprints (copper patterns for parts not fitted), so such additions stay possible later. All use JLCPCB-stocked parts.

🏎 Fast capture

ADS9224R dual 16-bit 3 MSPS, or an AD9235 40 MSPS on the free LCD_CAM parallel port: capture fast transients — the tier the 2026 core dropped for cost and time.

〰 DDS sine source

AD9833 direct digital synthesis to 1 MHz on the expansion header's SPI: complements the 16-bit AWG and pushes the source's frequency ceiling up.

🧮 Precision ADC

ADS1115 / ADS1256: 16–24 real bits for µV-level and sensor measurements. See what the MCU's own ADC can't — then prove it with a noise-floor plot.

🔢 More channels

MCP23017 (+16 GPIO) and CD74HC4067 (16 analog inputs into one pin): beyond an Arduino Mega with two cheap chips on one bus.

🎚 Programmable gain / autorange

PGA113 or digipot-set gain: an input stage that re-ranges itself so weak and strong signals both land on scale. The class board reserves an unpopulated PGA113 footprint on inputs 7–8 — fit it and write the driver.

⚖️ Instrumentation-amp input

INA826/INA333-class differential front-end: bridges, thermocouples and small signals riding on noisy grounds — measured cleanly. An INA826 footprint is reserved on the class board's conditioning zone.

🌡 Precision temperature channel

MAX31865 + PT100/PT1000 or a thermistor bridge: mK-resolution thermometry — the sensing half of a lab PID.

🔥 Heater / TEC driver

MOSFET PWM stage with INA current sense: the actuating half — closed-loop temperature control of a laser mount or sample stage.

🚶 Motion driver

A4988/DRV8825 stepper driver or servo headers: shutters, filter wheels, choppers — motion under your trigger engine.

⏰ Real timebase

TCXO (±1–2 ppm) and an external 10 MHz reference input: turn the generator into a metrology-grade source — then measure the improvement by beating two boards together. The class board carries an unpopulated TCXO footprint.

🔗 Isolated RS-485 / CAN

A proper multi-drop instrument bus across the lab, galvanically isolated — how real facilities wire their boxes together.

🖥 Touch-screen front panel

The phone/web app is the instrument's face and a small OLED on the class board shows status; if you want a stand-alone panel, add a 3.5″ 480×320 TFT (ILI9488, SPI) with capacitive touch (FT6236/GT911, I²C — another chip-to-chip bus) on the front-panel PCB, driven by LVGL. Lightweight version: OLED + rotary encoder.

💾 Logging + clock

microSD slot + DS3231 RTC: weeks of time-stamped data with no host PC — retrieve it over WiFi when you visit.

🔋 Battery power

TP4056 charger + boost converter: portable field measurements, and logging that survives a power cut.

💡 Photodiode / TIA input

A transimpedance light-measurement channel: laser power monitor, fluorescence pickup — analog design where layout genuinely matters. An OPA2156 TIA footprint is reserved on the class board's conditioning zone.

⚡ Power monitor

INA226 on your own rails: the instrument measures its own current, voltage and energy — self-diagnostics for free.

🔊 Audio-band codec

I²S ADC/DAC: a hi-fi analog path that upgrades the digital-filter and software lock-in directions to 24-bit quality.

🛡 More switching

Extra relays or high-side switches with proper flyback and isolation: grow the alarm/interlock fan-out to a whole rack.

Beyond the baseline: build something great

The presentation and demonstration (26–30 October, about 15 minutes each) has the same suggested structure for everyone (below). The software, the firmware, the app and everything around the instrument are yours to push as far as you like — pick what would genuinely help your own lab, or simply what you would be proud to show:

📱 Phone app / web UI

The ESP32 serves a polished web app (installable PWA — a web page that behaves like an app): waveform controls, live readings, dark mode, multi-user. Control your instrument from the seminar room.

🔔 Monitoring, alarms & relays

Threshold rules on the analog inputs: trip a relay, latch an interlock, push a Telegram/LINE alert. Fail-safe defaults, manual reset, event log with timestamps.

⏱ Triggers & sequencing

Trigger input → waveform burst or relay action; programmable sequences and schedules; sync two units. Turn the generator into an experiment conductor.

🐍 Python API & scripting

A clean instrument.sine(1e3, 0.5) API; automated sweeps; measure a filter's transfer function with your own generator + ADC and plot it.

📈 Calibration & characterisation

Calibrate amplitude and frequency; measure bandwidth, distortion, output impedance on the scope; publish a datasheet-style spec page. The physics content lives here.

🌐 Multi-instrument systems

MQTT or REST across several units; one dashboard for the whole lab; a classmate's box as a client. Systems engineering in miniature.

🛠 Firmware craft

Robust state machine, watchdog, settings that survive reboot, OTA (over-the-air, i.e. over WiFi) updates (ESP32), self-test on boot. The difference between a demo and a product.

🎛 Front-panel & enclosure polish

PCB front panel with silkscreen labels, light pipes, the OLED status display and — optionally — a touch UI (LVGL) that speaks the same API as your phone app; an enclosure that looks bought, not built.

🎬 Show the work

A video that introduces the board · a page of tips for working with AI, or with KiCad · a polished web page · a game that teaches something. Presenting well is a skill of its own. Just build something great.

Have a different idea from your own lab's needs? Great — put it in your spec. The board itself does not change.

The demonstration — a suggested structure

The presentation and demonstration takes about 15 minutes each, 26–30 October. A suggested structure for the demonstration part:

  1. Generate a requested waveform (frequency + amplitude) and show it on the oscilloscope.
  2. Change the output from your chosen interface (phone / web page / Python / bot).
  3. Set an alarm threshold on an analog input; show the relay trip and the notification.
  4. Show your section: its app panel and one thing it does — and your project website.
  5. Anything you built beyond the baseline — a data logger, a LINE alert, a PID block, a Python sweep, the tool your lab needed.

Fallback: the firmware's simulation mode runs the app and every panel on the bare dev board — if the board is late or dead, you demonstrate that, no stigma.

Video: a video describing your app and board is welcome as part of the presentation (for example through the Project 1 pipeline: Markdown → slides → website → YouTube); it is optional, and it plays if the live demonstration misbehaves.
