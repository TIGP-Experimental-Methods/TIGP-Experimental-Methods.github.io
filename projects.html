The class instrument: function generator / AWG & lab controller

Everyone builds the same instrument platform — a bench "Swiss-army-knife" every lab can use. In 2026 it is one class board in five blocks, one block per student. The hardware is deliberately standardised so the lectures, the design review and the class PCB order all pull in one direction; where you excel is the software: the interfaces, automation and polish that turn a circuit board into an instrument people love.

What the box does

  • Generates signals: a 16-bit two-channel arbitrary-waveform generator — DC levels, ramps, sines and arbitrary tables to about 50 kHz at ±10 V (AO1, AO2) — plus two hardware-timed trigger/clock outputs at 5 V TTL to tens of MHz.
  • Monitors signals: eight 16-bit ±10 V inputs on SMA connectors (AI1–AI8), with software-selectable ranges down to ±0.64 V for sensor work, logged and plotted. An instrumentation-amplifier / programmable-gain conditioning chain is reserved on the board as an instructor option feeding inputs 7 and 8.
  • Switches things: four low-voltage relays, eight 5 V TTL outputs, two fast opto-isolated inputs and a firmware-switchable TRIG connector (in or out) — alarms, interlocks, sequenced actions.
  • Talks to everything: native USB to Python, WiFi to a web page, your phone and messaging bots. Your phone is the front panel (a small OLED shows status); an optional camera node streams pictures into the same app.

MCU choice (frozen for 2026): an ESP32-S3 N16R8 dev board with the standard DevKitC-1 pinout and an external-antenna connector (Jinhua, NT$400), socketed on the class board and running the Arduino framework. WiFi unlocks the web/phone/bot directions; the u.FL antenna goes to an SMA bulkhead so WiFi works inside a closed metal or acrylic case; the dev board leaves 31 usable GPIO and an 80 MHz SPI bus for the converters. The core circuit is standardised so the class order and design reviews stay manageable — but you still do real hardware work on it (next paragraph), and your creativity also goes into firmware, host software, interfaces and the enclosure.

One class board, five blocks — you own one. The board is a single KiCad project in one class repo. The instructor builds the complete board first — the base (dev-board socket, buses, expansion header, the front-panel PCB with its 12 SMA connectors) and all five blocks — and hands you the full schematic as a PDF plus a gapped copy of your block's sheet with a few parts deleted. The blocks: precision inputs (ADS8688, AI1–AI8), power & rails (USB-C / 5 V jack, ±12 V isolated modules, regulators), signal generation (DAC8563 + ±10 V output stages), isolated switching (relays + fast opto inputs) and digital I/O & timing (TTL outputs, fast trigger outputs, the TRIG connector). Your work, each step done once with Claude as your tutor: place the deleted parts from the PDF, fetch one part from LCSC into the library, route your zone of the board (ICs and connectors are pre-placed), open a pull request and review one classmate's, then write your block's firmware driver and app panel from the template — in simulation mode first, on the real board when it arrives. The merged design goes into the class order on Mon 28 Sep and is manufactured five times — one assembled instrument each. Homework is capped at about 10 hours in total (3 h a week for three weeks + 1 h wrap-up); your zone, your PR and your review of a peer are what is assessed.

Rules: low-voltage only — the instrument runs from USB-C or a 5 V supply, relays switch signal-level and ≤ 30 V DC loads, never mains. Parts JLCPCB-stocked; dev board provided by the course. Cost: the class board is one design manufactured ×5, and the parts were chosen for learning per dollar — about NT$3.2k per student (≈ US$100) all-in: dev board, parts, 4-layer PCB, front-panel PCB and complete assembly by JLCPCB, so there is nothing to solder. Every part must earn its place, and the JLC quote is checked before the order.

Make it yours in hardware: the option-block menu

Hardware, like software, is where an instrument becomes yours — after the course, or as a stretch if you are clearly ahead. The class board carries a 2×10 expansion header, two Qwiic connectors and reserved footprints so it can grow; the 2026 core is frozen for the class order, and everything below is an option, not a deliverable (the 10-hour budget has no room for an extra block). Pick something your own lab would actually use. All suggestions use JLCPCB-stocked parts:

🏎 Fast capture

Option. ADS9224R dual 16-bit 3 MSPS, or an AD9235 40 MSPS on the free LCD_CAM parallel port: capture fast transients — the tier the 2026 core dropped for cost and time.

〰 DDS sine source

Option. AD9833 direct digital synthesis to 1 MHz on the expansion header's SPI: complements the 16-bit AWG and pushes the source's frequency ceiling up.

🧮 Precision ADC

ADS1115 / ADS1256: 16–24 real bits for µV-level and sensor measurements. See what the MCU's own ADC can't — then prove it with a noise-floor plot.

🔢 More channels

MCP23017 (+16 GPIO) and CD74HC4067 (16 analog inputs into one pin): beyond an Arduino Mega with two cheap chips on one bus.

🎚 Programmable gain / autorange

PGA113 or digipot-set gain: an input stage that re-ranges itself so weak and strong signals both land on scale. The class board reserves an unpopulated PGA113 footprint on inputs 7–8 — fit it and write the driver.

⚖️ Instrumentation-amp input

INA826/INA333-class differential front-end: bridges, thermocouples and small signals riding on noisy grounds — measured cleanly. An INA826 footprint is reserved on the class board's conditioning zone.

🌡 Precision temperature channel

MAX31865 + PT100/PT1000 or a thermistor bridge: mK-resolution thermometry — the sensing half of a lab PID.

🔥 Heater / TEC driver

MOSFET PWM stage with INA current sense: the actuating half — closed-loop temperature control of a laser mount or sample stage.

🚶 Motion driver

A4988/DRV8825 stepper driver or servo headers: shutters, filter wheels, choppers — motion under your trigger engine.

⏰ Real timebase

TCXO (±1–2 ppm) and an external 10 MHz reference input: turn the generator into a metrology-grade source — then measure the improvement by beating two boards together. The class board carries an unpopulated TCXO footprint.

🔗 Isolated RS-485 / CAN

A proper multi-drop instrument bus across the lab, galvanically isolated — how real facilities wire their boxes together.

🖥 Touch-screen front panel

Option block. The phone/web app is the instrument's face and a small OLED on the class board shows status; if you want a stand-alone panel, add a 3.5″ 480×320 TFT (ILI9488, SPI) with capacitive touch (FT6236/GT911, I²C) on the front-panel PCB, driven by LVGL. Lightweight version: OLED + rotary encoder.

💾 Logging + clock

microSD slot + DS3231 RTC: weeks of time-stamped data with no host PC — retrieve it over WiFi when you visit.

🔋 Battery power

TP4056 charger + boost converter: portable field measurements, and logging that survives a power cut.

💡 Photodiode / TIA input

A transimpedance light-measurement channel: laser power monitor, fluorescence pickup — analog design where layout genuinely matters. An OPA2156 TIA footprint is reserved on the class board's conditioning zone.

⚡ Power monitor

INA226 on your own rails: the instrument measures its own current, voltage and energy — self-diagnostics for free.

🔊 Audio-band codec

I²S ADC/DAC: a hi-fi analog path that upgrades the digital-filter and software lock-in directions to 24-bit quality.

🛡 More switching

Extra relays or high-side switches with proper flyback and isolation: grow the alarm/interlock fan-out to a whole rack.

Make it yours: the software excellence menu

The minimum demo (below) is the same for everyone. Distinction comes from how far you push one or two of these directions — pick what would genuinely help your own lab:

📱 Phone app / web UI

The ESP32 serves a polished web app (installable PWA): waveform controls, live readings, dark mode, multi-user. Control your instrument from the seminar room.

🔔 Monitoring, alarms & relays

Threshold rules on the analog inputs: trip a relay, latch an interlock, push a Telegram/LINE alert. Fail-safe defaults, manual reset, event log with timestamps.

⏱ Triggers & sequencing

Trigger input → waveform burst or relay action; programmable sequences and schedules; sync two units. Turn the generator into an experiment conductor.

🐍 Python API & scripting

A clean instrument.sine(1e3, 0.5) API; automated sweeps; measure a filter's transfer function with your own generator + ADC and plot it.

📈 Calibration & characterisation

Calibrate amplitude and frequency; measure bandwidth, distortion, output impedance on the scope; publish a datasheet-style spec page. The physics content lives here.

🌐 Multi-instrument systems

MQTT or REST across several units; one dashboard for the whole lab; a classmate's box as a client. Systems engineering in miniature.

🛠 Firmware craft

Robust state machine, watchdog, settings that survive reboot, OTA updates (ESP32), self-test on boot. The difference between a demo and a product.

🎛 Front-panel & enclosure polish

PCB front panel with silkscreen labels, light pipes, the OLED status display and — optionally — a touch UI (LVGL) that speaks the same API as your phone app; an enclosure that looks bought, not built.

Have a different software direction from your own lab's needs? Great — pitch it in your spec; it needs instructor sign-off only if it changes the board.

Minimum demo (the same short checklist for everyone)

  1. Generate a requested waveform (frequency + amplitude) and show it on the oscilloscope.
  2. Change the output from your chosen interface (phone / web page / Python / bot).
  3. Set an alarm threshold on an analog input; show the relay trip and the notification.
  4. Show your block: its app panel and one thing it does — and your page in the class repo.

Fallback: the firmware's simulation mode runs the app and every panel on the bare dev board from day 1 — if the board is late or dead, you demo that, no stigma; your 60-second video is the second safety net.

Video: in the wrap-up week, record a 60-second phone video of your panel moving real hardware. It goes on your page in the class repo, is linked from the Showcase, and plays if the live demo misbehaves.

Fabrication lead time is the hard constraint: PCB fabrication, assembly and shipping to Taiwan take about 2 weeks (boards back ≈ 12 Oct), so the Monday 28 September class-order cutoff is non-negotiable. The boxes are printed in-house by the instructor — no second order.

Demo week (26–30 October 2026)

Demo slots are scheduled individually during the week of 26–30 October (confirmed in class). You demo to the class and invited PIs, in English — using AI to prepare is encouraged, and no preparation beyond the wrap-up hour is budgeted.

  • 5-minute demo from the class checklist: a waveform out, seen on the scope and on the instrument's own input · change it from the phone · alarm on an input → relay → notification · your block: its panel and one thing it does.
  • Your page in the class repo (docs/students/<name>/): your SPEC, progress notes, your pull requests, your box STL and your video — linked from the Showcase.
  • Your 60-second video from the wrap-up week — plays if the live demo misbehaves.
  • Score sheet: block works 40 % · pull-request quality, including your review of a peer, 30 % · demo clarity 20 % · box 10 %.
