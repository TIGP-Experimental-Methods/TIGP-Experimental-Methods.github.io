Build a real lab instrument in one month

A hands-on short course for graduate students in experimental physics: three workshops, then a presentation and demonstration of your project.

  • Workshop 1: AI for experimentalists — how large language models work, the tools we use (VS Code, the Claude Code extension, git, free hosting), our "AI method" in 5 steps, and two projects: Project 1, a web app of your choice (a simulator or a game) deployed on the public web, and Project 2, a phone app that controls and measures from the ESP32 (a small WiFi microcontroller board, provided) over WiFi.
  • Workshop 2: Designing printed circuit boards — a crash course in electronics, then Project 3a: turning our ESP32 into a real piece of lab equipment. Together we build the skeleton of the class board in KiCad (the free program we draw circuits and lay out boards in); everyone designs a different section, so as a team we produce a powerful instrument. The board is manufactured and assembled by JLCPCB.
  • Workshop 3: Firmware and basic mechanical design (PCB housing) — a tour of the IAMS fabrication facilities, CAD basics, and Project 3b: a housing for your instrument (3D printing and/or laser cutting), while you continue building your app and firmware.
  • Presentation and demonstration (26–30 October) — collect the assembled boards and housings, assemble, test, then present and demonstrate your project, app, website and, if you like, a video.

The class board plugs onto the ESP32-S3 dev board (a small computer-on-a-chip with WiFi, on a board with a USB-C connector and pins) and gives it what the dev board cannot do: 2 × ±10 V 16-bit waveform outputs, 8 × ±10 V 16-bit inputs, 4 relays, 2 isolated fast inputs, 8 TTL outputs (5 V logic-level signals) and 2 fast trigger outputs, a switchable TRIG, a phone app, a Python API (a set of Python commands for driving the board from a PC) and an OLED (a small display) — all from one USB-C cable. Can you adapt it to solve a problem in your current research?

The message of the course: effective use of AI is a superpower for an experimentalist — if you can specify what you want and verify what you get. Never trust AI without testing and verifying.

Before the first class, complete the Preparation steps. Bring your laptop, fully prepared; the ESP32 board is provided.

Course objective

Teach the key skills needed in every modern experimental physics lab.

Intelligence

Software and AI: an AI coding agent that plans, writes, tests and deploys the programs you specify — and how to work with it reliably.

Electronics

Electronics is how we interface intelligence with the real world: a printed circuit board that gives a microcontroller precise inputs, outputs and switching.

Mechanics

Creating physical hardware — CAD, 3D printing and laser cutting — so the board becomes an instrument you can put on a bench.

We are not teaching how AI works or electronic/mechanical engineering (that takes years). We aim to give you a crash course in using the tools you need to get started with your own projects.

How the course works

One project, three workshops

The workshops are not separate topics — they are the three skills needed to build one device: the class instrument. Your web app, your phone app, your section of the board and your housing all end up in your project.

Workshop, not seminar

Each workshop is a short introduction followed by building, with a Claude tutor (/tutor in Claude Code) at your side, in English or Mandarin. Theory appears when it unblocks a decision — ask the tutor to explain an idea, and let it ask you questions back.

The "AI method" in 5 steps

Vision → Specification/Plan → Review → Code → Test/Debug/Verify, then iterate: the first plan is never the last plan. We use VS Code + the Claude Code extension, with git as the safety net. Never trust AI without testing and verifying.

Do each step once, for real

Deploy a real app, design a real section of a real board, draw a real housing — and your section goes into a professionally assembled board you demonstrate in its housing. Be creative.

What you will have built by the end

  • A deployed web app — a simulator or a game — with its report explaining the concept.
  • A phone app controlling your ESP32 over WiFi.
  • Your section of the class board, designed in KiCad and manufactured.
  • A housing for the instrument, 3D-printed and/or laser-cut.
  • The presentation and demonstration of your project, its website — and, optionally, a video describing your app and board.

Each project lives in its own public GitHub repository under your account and has its own project website (GitHub Pages: what it is, how it works, a picture or video of it working, what was verified, links to the app and the code). All of them appear on the class project wall — on the projector in class, refreshing itself every minute, so everyone sees each other's projects appear. See Showcase.

The hardware is the shared baseline. The software, firmware and app are open — a data logger, a LINE alert, a PID block, a Python sweep, the tool your lab needs. That is where you show what you can do. Talk to your instrument from LINE or Telegram — the extension we most encourage: push notifications when a value drifts or an alarm fires, and bot commands that read a value or switch a relay from your phone, from anywhere. Just build something great.
