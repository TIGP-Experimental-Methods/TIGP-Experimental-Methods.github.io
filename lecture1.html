AI 101 — build it, ship it, log it — then your instrument

Goal. By the break you have built an inverted-pendulum simulator with an AI coding agent from a five-line spec you wrote yourself, checked it against physics, put it on the public web with your name on it, and written your first Markdown log entry. By the end of the two hours your own phone app talks to the ESP32 in your hand — one button that acts on the board, one live number coming back — flashed by you, verified on your phone, shipped as a pull request. You also know which block of the class board is yours.

Learning goals: work with an AI coding agent from a spec you wrote · verify AI output with a physical check, not by looking at it · put a program on the public web and open it on your phone · keep a Markdown log the next session can resume from · build a phone app that talks to your ESP32, flash it, ship it as a PR · know your block.

Why we start away from the project. AI-assisted work is a general skill. The same three moves serve a simulation, a website, a video, a report, a KiCad board and a firmware driver: build something from a written spec, ship it where others can see it, log what you did in plain text. Today you practise the three moves twice: first on something every physicist already understands — a pendulum — with no hardware in the way; then on the ESP32 in your hand, with a phone app of your own. Electronics design is one application of AI, not the syllabus.

The one-sentence thesis: an AI agent is a junior engineer with no memory — it is exactly as good as the project documentation, the task specification and the verification you give it. Everything below follows from that sentence.

Prerequisite: the preparation checklist (workbook ch. 0, at most one hour, not counted in the 10 hours of homework) — including a free Cloudflare account connected to GitHub. Bring your laptop and a USB cable; the dev board (ESP32-S3) is handed to you at the start, already flashed with the skeleton — you flash your own build at 15:35, so the PlatformIO toolchain must be downloaded at home.

How we work: your own repo for your own things, one class repo for the instrument, a tutor on every screen

Build

A written spec first — five lines, yours. Then a plan, then code. Today: an inverted pendulum in one index.html (canvas + JavaScript, no framework, no build). The agent writes the boilerplate; you set the parameters and you do the check.

Ship

Your own GitHub repo pendulum, deployed on Cloudflare Pages straight from git — no build command, a public *.pages.dev URL, redeployed on every push. GitHub Pages is the fallback. This repo becomes your personal course page.

Log — in Markdown

PROGRESS.md at the end of every session: done / verified / next / gotchas. Plain text next to the code: it diffs, it is searchable, the AI reads it at the start of the next session, and it is your lab notebook for this course. Specs, logs, notes, READMEs, slides — Markdown is the lingua franca of AI work.

The Claude tutor

In Claude Code, /tutor L1 runs today's workbook chapter with you, in English or Mandarin: it checks your toolchain, paces every step, refuses to write code before your SPEC.md exists, asks "what did you measure?" before you commit, and writes your PROGRESS.md with you. Later: /tutor HW1, /tutor L2, …

The instrument — the same moves on hardware

One class repo TIGP-Experimental-Methods/class-board-2026: firmware/ (PlatformIO), host/ (phone app + instrument.py), hardware/ (KiCad), workbook/, tutor/ and your folder docs/students/<name>/. Firmware on the ESP32-S3 ↔ WebSocket ↔ a phone web app ↔ Python. The board is its own WiFi access point. main is protected; you work on branches and pull requests; the instructor merges.

Your block

The class board has five student blocks — B1 inputs · B2 power · B3 outputs · B4 switching · B5 digital/TRIG — and five students. At the end of today each of you owns one: you read it, route it, write its driver and demo it.

The five working practices (the spine of the course)

  1. Spec first. Requirements live in a file (SPEC.md), not in chat scrollback; ask for a plan before code. Today's spec is five lines long and you write it yourself.
  2. Teach the repo, not the session: CLAUDE.md. Conventions, pinout, build/flash commands, how to test — written once, loaded every session.
  3. Small, fresh sessions. One task = one session; finish, commit, start clean. Long sessions rot.
  4. Handover notes — in Markdown. Every session ends by updating PROGRESS.md: done / verified / next / gotchas. Lab-notebook discipline applied to AI work.
  5. AI writes, you verify. No verification plan → the output is not yours to ship. In this course "verify" means a number or an observation: does the swing period match 2π√(L/g)? what do you see on the phone / in DRC / on the scope?

The class repo is built the same way: the course documents, the workbook and the tutor skill are the course's own CLAUDE.md and handover log.

Homework 1 (assessed, 2.5 h) — Exercise E2 (finish) + E3–E5

  • E2 finish + merge (0.5 h). If your app did not fully work in class, finish it with /tutor HW1 (Wednesday office hour if flashing is the problem). The instructor reviews PRs on Monday; address one comment and merge. Teaches: the PR loop.
  • E3 (0.5 h) Read your block. The tutor shows your block's PDF page and asks two questions (B1 "why is there a 1 kΩ in series with each input?" · B2 "what happens if USB and the jack are both plugged in?" · B3 "what sets the ±10 V swing?" · B4 "what does the diode across the relay coil do?" · B5 "why can't a GPIO drive 5 V TTL directly?"). Answers go in docs/students/<name>/notes.md. Watch V3. Teaches: schematic reading, the purpose of your block.
  • E4 (0.5 h) KiCad ready. Install KiCad 10; unzip the library package into the repo; open class-board.kicad_pro; find your rule area; screenshot it. Watch V4. Teaches: KiCad orientation.
  • E5 (0.5 h) Your SPEC paragraph. One paragraph in docs/students/<name>/SPEC.md: what your block must do, one number, how you will show it on demo day — the pendulum spec, one level up. Teaches: spec first.
  • Videos V3 + V4 (16 min) and the workbook ch. 1 reading fill the rest of the 2.5 h. Every work session ends with a PROGRESS.md entry and a commit — in your pendulum repo and in the class repo alike. This rule holds for the whole course.

Assessed at the end of the week: your pendulum URL and its PROGRESS.md (from class), the merged E2 (your app), the two answers in notes.md, the zone screenshot and SPEC.md — all in a repo, all by commit or PR.

Where the three moves go next

  • Build: your part of the board (Lecture 2), your block's driver and app panel (Lecture 3, Firmware and Mechanical 101) — the E2 app grown into a real block panel; same spec → plan → boilerplate → you edit the lines that matter → verify.
  • Ship: the class board is ordered on 28 Sep from everyone's pull requests; your panel ships in the instrument's app; your pendulum site is already public.
  • Log: PROGRESS.md at the end of every session; SPEC.md grows a measured number in the wrap-up.
  • Your personal page: the pendulum repo is your course page. In the wrap-up you add your 60-second video and your measured number to it; the Showcase links to it next to your block. Same site, more on it every week — that is "show your work".
  • Videos, slides, reports with AI (optional, from Lecture 3): the lab's show-your-work skills for Claude Code — education-video, slide-deck, technical-report, natural-voice, showoff-render — install into any repo with python tools/install_skills.py <repo>. Use them for your 60-second video or your demo slides if you like; a phone video is equally acceptable.
